When my grandpa died, we sorted through his belongings and found his old
primary school report card which was covered with swastikas and skulls. That was
the world he grew up in. 

He was a little older than ten when Konrad Zuse built the first functioning programmable digital computer. Later in his life, the first graphical user interfaces was created, the Macintosh was introduced, the internet became a thing, and smartphones moved into almost every pocket.

**And yet, in more than ninety years of life, he never used a personal computer.**

Today, computers are impossible to avoid. Anyone who wants to participate
in modern life is sooner or later forced to use these machines. They are no
longer tools for specialists, engineers, or nerdy hobbyists. Even people who
do not think of themselves as "computer people" use them every day and lead a
digital life.

With this history in mind, my computer becomes the most valuable thing I own
because it stores all the things I spend my time on: my blog post ramblings,
my code, my lecture slides, my photos, my unfinished ideas, my half-organized
folders. All of it somehow exists as imaginary files, encoded somewhere as
zeros and ones.

I want to push the analogy a little further.

Just like bicycles, computers come in different forms for different people and
different purposes. Some people need a stable touring bike that can carry a
lot of weight and survive bad weather conditions. Others want an elegant road
bike that is optimized for speed and design. And then there are people like me
who are looking for something closer to a gravel bike: a mostly stable and
fast option that is ready for whatever road it encounters.

And then there are people who can ride across a continent on a rusty old bike
with bad brakes and still arrive before you have finished researching the
perfect frame material. I respect this “Richard Stallman mindset” a lot, but
it is just not feasible for the normal population.

It reminds me of [this funny YouTube video I found a while ago](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cZk2jV5gJbM&t=2s).

The right machine depends on what you want to do with it. Once you have the
right base, you should take some time to personalize and customize it.

### Gear does matter

If you work in an office or study at a university, you will spend multiple
hours every day in front of a screen. The computer becomes the interface
between you and almost everything you do. It is hard to describe, but over
this interface datasets become kind of tangible, a quality that pen and pencil
cannot provide.

You do not need the newest hardware, and you do not need the most expensive
machine. But your machine should feel like something you
actually want to touch. Personally, I strongly prefer
aluminium or other rigid materials over cheap plastic, because the machine
feels less like a disposable object and more like a tool.

That is why I decided to go with a dual-boot setup.

In bicycle terms, Windows is the smooth road: stable, predictable, and boring
in a useful way. It lets you enjoy the landscape without thinking too much
about the ground underneath you. Linux is more like the rough gravel path:
more hands-on, sometimes a bit bumpy, but it lets you go almost anywhere if
you are willing to learn how to ride it.

Because I had already spent some time thinking about what I actually needed
from my machine, the choice became more concrete. I wanted a laptop that could
daily-drive Linux, but still give me full Windows compatibility whenever
university work required it.

That meant I needed a machine with good Linux support and enough storage
flexibility for a clean dual-boot setup. In the end, I chose one of the newer
ThinkPads with two SSD slots, so Windows and Linux could each live on their
own drive instead of awkwardly sharing one.

The configuration I bought was:

* AMD Ryzen 7 7735HS
* Windows 11 Home
* 32 GB DDR5-4800 RAM
* 1 TB M.2 2242 PCIe 4.0 SSD
* 16-inch WQXGA display, 2560 × 1600
* matte IPS panel
* 100% sRGB
* 400 nits
* low blue light

I bought the laptop during Black Week and paid **766.42 €** for it. The
original price for my configuration was **1,239.00 €**, but the education
discount and the Black Week coupon reduced the price by **472.58 €**. After
that, I ordered another 1 TB SSD for around 100 € and installed Linux on the
second drive.

For my use case, this was almost perfect. Windows stays available for
university software, Microsoft Office, Teams, and everything that expects the
standard corporate operating system. Linux gets its own clean space for
programming, writing, learning, and experimenting.

### bike fitting
If you spend a lot of time on a bike, you eventually need a bike fitting. The
saddle height, handlebar position, tire pressure, pedals, and grips all start
to matter. Small things become annoying when you repeat them thousands of
times.

If you only use a computer every few weeks, the most important thing is that
it is simple and forgiving. But many people today are knowledge workers,
students, programmers, writers, designers, or office workers. They spend hours
every day in front of these machines. Instead of “user-friendliness for
beginners.” the goal should be a reliable interface that lets you become
fluent.

This is also what interests me about human-computer interaction. I do not
think the future of computing is mainly weird hand gestures, VR glasses, or
waving your fingers in the air like a wizard. For daily work I want something
much more boring, which is an interface that reacts quickly, precisely, and
predictably.

These are the modifications I made:

* **I swapped `Fn` and `Ctrl`.**
  I really dislike the default placement of the `Fn` and `Ctrl` keys on
  ThinkPad keyboards. Thankfully, Lenovo lets you swap them in the BIOS. If
  that had not been possible, it would have driven me insane.

* **I stayed with a German keyboard layout.**
  I thought about getting an English keyboard because it is nicer for
  programming. But this machine is also for writing my theses in German,
  writing messages in German, and generally living in a German-speaking
  environment. So the German layout makes more sense for me.

* **I turned `Caps Lock` into `Escape`.**
  The `Caps Lock` key is one of the strangest keys on a modern keyboard. It
  gets a huge amount of space for a function I almost never use. For my
  workflow, especially in the terminal and in editors, `Escape` is much more
  useful. So `Caps Lock` became `Escape`.

* **I mostly ignore the Copilot key.**
  The most useless key on the machine is probably the Copilot key. I
  understand why it exists from a marketing perspective, but I do not need a
  dedicated key for that. If I cared enough, I would remap it or physically
  remove it out of spite.
